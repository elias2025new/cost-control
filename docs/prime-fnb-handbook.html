01

Why this exists at all

A restaurant buys food, cooks it, and sells it. Simple. The hard question is the one nobody can answer at the end of the month: did we actually make the money we think we made?

Imagine you run a shop that sells lemonade. You buy lemons and sugar. You sell cups. At the end of the week you count your money and you count your lemons. If you sold 100 cups, you should have used a certain number of lemons. If far more lemons are gone than that, something is wrong — someone spilled a crate, or someone is making cups for their friends, or your recipe is really using twice the lemons you think.

A restaurant is that, times about five hundred. Prime sells 171 different things. In one 27-day stretch they sold 15,983 items for 8,928,373.06 birr. Behind those sales, hundreds of kilos of beef, teff, butter and coffee left the store. Nobody wrote down a single one of those movements, because nobody could.

The four places money quietly leaves

  • Waste — food spoils, gets dropped, gets burnt.
  • Voids — an order is rung up then cancelled, but the food was already made.
  • Complimentary — a dish given away to a guest. A real plate left the kitchen.
  • Staff meals — the team eats. Real ingredients, no revenue.

Each of these is legitimate. The problem is not that they happen; it is that nobody measures them, so they hide everything else — including theft and over-portioning.

What they use today, and why it breaks

Ato Eyob's team runs a spreadsheet. It is a genuinely good spreadsheet: item master, recipes, sub-recipes, wastage log, variance sheets, a monthly reconciliation. It has 23 tabs.

Spreadsheets like it die the same three deaths.

  • History gets rewritten. Butter goes up in price, so someone types the new price into the cost cell. Every report ever produced from that sheet silently changes. Last month's food cost is now a different number than it was last month. Nobody can ever reconcile anything again.
  • Stock is a cell you edit. Someone types over a balance and the trail is gone. There is no way to ask "how did we get to 102 kilos?"
  • The arithmetic quietly drifts. In their real workbook, every dish that uses a sub-recipe is under-costed — the sub-recipe line is priced at zero. Their Classic Beef Burger is costed at 106.15 and simply leaves out its own bun. Continental Breakfast is costed at 16.80 with both of its sub-recipes missing.
The point

This system is not "the spreadsheet, but on a website". It is built so that those three failures are structurally impossible. That is the entire design brief.

02

The one big idea

If you understand nothing else, understand this. Everything else is detail.

A recipe says what should go into a dish. The till says how many of that dish were sold. Multiply the two together and you know what should have left the shelf. Then go and look at the shelf. The difference between those two numbers is the whole business of cost control.

ARecipeOne Yeajet Kitfo uses 0.25 kg of beef
BTillWe sold 1,392 of them
CShould have used348 kg of beef
DCount the shelfWhat is actually there
EThe gapThe thing worth money

Nobody sold beef. They sold kitfo. The system worked out the beef by itself, from the recipe, and moved 348 kilos out of the kitchen without a human typing anything. That is the trick, and once you have seen it, the rest of the system is bookkeeping around it.

Two different questions

There are actually two gaps worth measuring, and the system keeps them apart on purpose.

Inventory variance — what the books say is on the shelf, against what you counted. Catches theft and miscounting.

Theoretical consumption — what the recipes say should have been used, against what the store actually handed out. Catches recipe drift and over-portioning: the chef using 300 grams where the recipe says 250.

They sound similar and they are not. Merging them would hide both. The first has a screen today; the second is computed by the engine and has no screen yet.

03

Every word you need, explained once

This is the vocabulary. Learn these thirteen words and every screen makes sense.

Item

A raw thing you buy. Beef, teff flour, butter, coffee beans, a bottle of water. It has a code (RM-BEEF-KITFO), a unit (Kg, Ltr, Bottle) and a cost. There are 26 of them seeded. Nothing a guest ever orders is an item.

Menu item

A thing a guest can order. Yeajet Kitfo. Macchiato. Mojito. This is what the recipe belongs to.

Article

What the till calls it. Prime's POS is called CNET, and inside CNET every button is an "article" with a code like P-00001. The article is the bridge between the till and the menu item.

Why the code matters and the name does not

In Prime's own export there are two articles both called MOJITO. One is P-00257 at 1,490.68 birr. The other is P-00402, spelled " MOJITO" with a leading space, at 1,614.91. Twenty-one article names carry stray spaces. There are two different SPECIAL TEAs.

If the system matched on names it would merge two different drinks at two different prices and quietly corrupt everything downstream. So it matches on the code, always, and treats the code as text — because three real codes have no P- prefix at all (00081, 00040, 00039) and their leading zeros must survive.

Recipe

A list of what goes into one portion. Yeajet Kitfo needs beef, ayib, gomen and injera.

Sub-recipe

A recipe inside a recipe. Nobody buys niter kibbeh — the kitchen makes a batch of it from butter and spices, and then a spoonful of that batch goes into several dishes.

You cost the whole batch once, divide by how much the batch makes, and pull that cost-per-unit into the parent dish. Recipes are therefore a tree, not a flat list.

Yeajet Kitfo — 407.18 birr a plate ├─ Kitfo Base (batch makes 4 portions, 1,242.11 a batch) │ ├─ Beef, lean minced 1 kg │ ├─ Niter Kibbeh (batch makes 2.7 kg, 2,789.00 a batch) │ │ ├─ Butter 3 kg │ │ ├─ Ginger 0.05 kg │ │ ├─ Garlic 0.05 kg │ │ ├─ Korarima 0.02 kg │ │ └─ Besobela 0.02 kg │ ├─ Mitmita 0.02 kg │ └─ Salt 0.005 kg ├─ Ayib 0.08 kg ├─ Gomen 0.10 kg └─ Injera (batch makes 12 pieces, 390.30 a batch) ├─ Teff flour 3 kg └─ Salt 0.01 kg ← salt arrives twice, and is added up once

Three levels deep. Notice salt: it comes in through Kitfo Base and through Injera. The system adds those two together into a single line of 0.002917 kg rather than moving stock twice. Niter Kibbeh is also pulled straight into Fillet Tibes, so one sub-recipe feeds two dishes at two different depths.

Store

A place stock physically sits. There are three: Main Store (the lock-up), Outlet Kitchen, Outlet Bar. Goods arrive into Main and get issued out to the Kitchen and Bar, which is where sales deplete from.

Stock movement

The single most important idea in the whole build. Every time stock changes, the system writes one new line in a list: what item, which store, plus or minus how much, at what cost, on what date, and why.

Stock on hand is never stored anywhere. It is always the sum of that list. Ask "how much beef is in the kitchen?" and the answer is worked out by adding up every line that ever touched beef in the kitchen.

Like a bank statement

Your bank does not store your balance and edit it. It stores every transaction and adds them up. You can always ask how you got here. This system works exactly the same way, and for exactly the same reason.

Moving weighted average cost

You bought 300 kg of beef at 1,100 birr, then another 300 kg at 1,150. What does a kilo cost now? The average, weighted by how much you bought: 1,125.00.

When beef leaves the kitchen for a sale, it leaves at 1,125 — not at the old price, not at the new one. Buying stock moves the average. Using stock does not.

Cost version

A price with a date range attached. Butter was 900 birr from 1 July. On 1 August it became 1,150. Both facts are kept forever, side by side. Nothing is overwritten.

Ask what Yeajet Kitfo cost on 15 July and you get 407.18. Ask what it costs on 15 August and you get 415.51. Both answers are correct, and the July answer will still be 407.18 in five years.

Depletion

The automatic removal of ingredients from a store when something is sold. You post the day's sales; the recipes explode; the stock moves.

Coverage

The honest number. What percentage of the money taken could the system actually cost?

Right now there are 8 recipes against 171 articles, so coverage is 43.01%. The other 5,088,483.35 birr of sales has no recipe behind it, so the system says so plainly rather than pretending. As recipes get written, this climbs.

Theoretical vs. actual

Theoretical = what the books say should be there. Actual = what a human counted.

Variance, and the three words

Theoretical minus actual, in quantity and in birr, graded:

StatusWhenWhat it means
OKwithin 3%Normal. Scales and spoons are not perfect.
REVIEW3% – 8%Worth a question. Ask the section head.
INVESTIGATEover 8%Something is wrong. Find out what.

One exception: if the books say zero and you counted something, that is always INVESTIGATE. A percentage of nothing is meaningless.

04

The seven rules it will never break

These are not preferences or good intentions. Each one is enforced by the database itself, so no future change — including a careless one — can undo them.

Rule 1

Stock on hand is never stored. There is no balance column anywhere. Stock is always the sum of the movement list. You cannot corrupt a number that does not exist.

Rule 2

Nothing is edited or deleted after posting. The database physically refuses. Try to change a posted stock movement and it throws an error telling you to post a reversing entry instead. A correction is a new line, never an erasure.

Rule 3

Costs and recipes are versioned by date. Changing a price today cannot change last month's report. This is the single biggest failure of the spreadsheet and the main reason he is buying software.

Rule 4

Exact decimals, never floating point. Computers store 0.1 + 0.2 as 0.30000000000000004. Over 15,983 lines that becomes real money and an accountant finds it. Every figure here is fixed-point, and a test proves the code rounds the same way the database does — half away from zero, the way an accountant expects.

Rule 5

Every table is scoped to a tenant. Prime may add branches or companies later. The column and the discipline start now, even though there is one tenant today.

Rule 6

An import that does not reconcile does not post. The lines must add up to the total printed at the bottom of his own report — exactly, to the last decimal. If they don't, the button stays off.

Rule 7

Revenue always posts; depletion is best-effort. Every sale is recorded whether or not a recipe exists. Only lines with a recipe move stock; the rest are flagged as uncosted, never dropped. With 8 recipes against 171 articles, a system that refused to post until everything had a recipe would never post at all.

It reconciles to the last decimal

Add up all 171 article rows and you get 8,928,373.056. The Sub Total printed at the bottom of his report says 8,928,373.056. Not close — identical. Every category subtotal ties too, and so does the 15,983 quantity.

Why the third decimal matters

His amounts carry three decimal places. Stored at the usual two, the sum would no longer reproduce his footer and the system would refuse every real file he has. So POS amounts are held at four decimal places — still exact, still never a float. Figures the system calculates itself stay at two.

The service charge is not 10%

His written spec says service charge is 10% and VAT is 15% of the total after it. The worked example: an item at 100 birr carries 10 service charge, then VAT of 16.50 on the 110, for 126.50. The system reproduces that exactly.

But across the whole real file the add-on charge came to 4.9365% of net sales, not 10%. That means only about half of sales carry it — takeaway is the likely reason. So the system never applies a flat rate per line. It uses the figure printed in his file and flags the question for him.

Things that can never be costed

Six articles name a category rather than a product. No recipe can ever exist for "SOFT DRINK" because nobody knows which drink it was.

Together that is 328,374.47 birr — 3.68% of sales — that no amount of recipe writing will ever reach. Splitting those buttons in the POS is the only fix, and that is his decision to make.

Free drinks are real stock

Four articles — FREE WATER, FREE AMBO, FREE SOFT DRINK, FREE NOVIDA — total 80 units for 7.73 birr. That is not revenue. But the water physically left the building.

So they do two different things at once: they deplete stock and they are excluded from sales. Getting that backwards in either direction is a real bug, and there are tests on both halves.

A rounding difference worth reporting, not blocking

His Total Tax is 1,405,368.786. A flat 15% of net plus charge gives 1,405,368.024. A difference of 0.762 birr across 15,983 items — per-bill rounding inside the till. The system reports it and never blocks on it.

The file is a month, not a day

Depletion wants one business day. The file he actually sent covers 8 July to 3 August 2026 — 27 days. So the system records a period with a start and an end, and a single day is just the case where they are the same. It works with what he has rather than demanding what he doesn't.

06

The screens, one by one

Seven screens. Here is what each one is for, what you do on it, and what to look at.

Overview /

The front page. How many articles exist, how many are mapped to a recipe, how many stock movements have been recorded, and what every recipe costs today. Start here to get your bearings; you don't do any work on it.

Sales import /import

Where the day's takings come in. Drag the CNET spreadsheet onto the box and press Upload and validate.

Nothing posts on upload. The file is read, checked against its own totals, and handed to you for review. Below the box is every import ever uploaded, with whether it reconciled and how much of it could be costed.

Validation panel /import/<id>

The most important screen in the system, and the one to spend time on in a demo. Top to bottom:

  • Reconciliation — sum of lines, the footer total, and the difference. It should read 0.00.
  • Cost coverage — the percentage bar. Currently 43.01%.
  • Blocking problems — only appears when something structural is wrong. Posting is off while it is there.
  • Post to stock — the button. Grey and unclickable unless everything above is satisfied.
  • Will move stock / Stock moved — the 10 lines that have recipes.
  • Biggest gaps — the articles where writing one recipe would buy the most coverage. This is the to-do list.
  • Warnings — generic articles, test articles, free articles, unmapped articles. Grouped, counted, totalled. None of them stop the posting.
  • Notes on this file — the service charge observation, the tax rounding, the fact that voids are excluded from this report.
  • Approval routing — his three-level chain, drawn but greyed out. Shown so he can see it is understood, not pretended to work.

Stock ledger /ledger

Pick a store, pick an item, see every movement that ever touched it and the running balance after each one.

Try Outlet Kitchen → Beef, lean minced. You will see 450 kg issued in, then 348 kg depleted by the sale, leaving 102. Every row shows the unit cost it moved at.

Recipes & costs /recipes

Two jobs. It shows every recipe's cost on two dates side by side, and it lets you record a new ingredient cost from a chosen date.

Change butter, and the earlier column does not move. That is the whole argument of the system in one table. Underneath, cost history shows every version ever recorded with the window it applied to.

Physical count /counts

Choose a store and a date, press Start count, and the system builds a sheet listing every item that store holds, with the theoretical quantity already filled in.

Walk the shelves, type what is actually there, and add a reason where it differs. The status column grades itself as you type. Save entries, then Post count.

Why the sheet is frozen

The theoretical figure is snapshotted the moment the sheet is created. If it were recalculated at posting time, later deliveries would move the target and the variance would stop meaning anything. Once posted, the sheet cannot be edited at all — it is the only record of what was on the shelf that morning.

Variance /variance

Books against count, per item, sorted worst first by money — because that is what a controller actually looks at. Quantity difference, percentage, birr value, status, and the reason someone typed.

The summary strip splits shrinkage from overage, because they are different problems.

Dashboard /dashboard

Food cost and beverage cost, side by side and never merged. Each card shows the percentage, the revenue, and — importantly — the coverage it is dividing by.

07

The demo, click by click

Before you start

Stop the server, run npm run db:reset, start it again. Do not run npm run demo:import — that posts the file ahead of time, and then step 1 hits "period already posted" and the demo dies.

  1. Sales import. Drag reference/cnet_sales_export.xlsx in. Press Upload and validate. "This is your own export, untouched."
  2. Point at Reconciliation. "8,928,373.06 — the sum of the lines, and the total printed at the bottom of your report. Exact. If it didn't match, the button below stays off."
  3. Scroll to Cost coverage. "43% of your revenue can be costed today, with eight recipes. Five million birr can't. That number goes up as recipes get written — and this is the honest way to show it."
  4. Scroll to Biggest gaps. "SOFT DRINK: 1,803 units. That isn't a product, it's a category. No recipe can ever exist for it. Same for BEER."
  5. Press Post to stock. "Nothing was typed. Stock just moved."
  6. Press Post again. "Posting twice is safe. Nothing doubles."
  7. Stock ledger → Outlet Kitchen → Beef, lean minced. "450 kilos issued in, 348 gone. Nobody sold beef — they sold kitfo. The recipe did that."
  8. Physical count → Outlet Kitchen, date 2026-08-04 → Start count. "The sheet freezes what the books say right now."
  9. Type the counts. Beef 97, reason "Five kilos unaccounted for". Teff flour 1160. Save entries. "It grades itself as you type."
  10. Post count. "The difference becomes an adjustment. The original entry is never edited."
  11. Variance. "Sorted by money, worst first. That's what you'd actually look at."
  12. Dashboard. "Food 27.5%, beverage 22.1% — never merged. Each one tells you the coverage it's dividing by, so you always know what the number covers."
  13. Coverage curve, bottom of the dashboard. "One recipe gets you a quarter of your revenue. Five gets you half. Twenty-three gets you eighty percent. It's a finite job."
  14. Recipes & costs. Note Yeajet Kitfo at 407.18 on 15 July.
  15. Change butter to 1150, effective 2026-08-01. Record new cost. "Butter went up."
  16. Look at the two columns. "15 July still says 407.18. 15 August says 415.51. Your July report did not move. That is the thing the spreadsheet cannot do."
  17. Cost history → Butter. "Both versions kept. The old one closed, not overwritten."
Order matters

Steps 8–11 need step 5 first — no posted sales means no depletion, so a count finds nothing. The dashboard also reads the latest posted import. And step 15's date must be after 1 July 2026, because that is when the seeded costs begin; the form refuses an earlier date rather than rewriting history.

What is real and what we made up

This distinction matters more than anything else in the demo. Say it out loud before he asks.

Real — straight out of his own systems

  • All 171 article codes, names, categories and sub-categories
  • Every quantity and every price in the sales file
  • The branch, the dates, the footer totals, the tax and service charge figures
  • The 3% / 8% variance thresholds, from his workbook
  • The approval chain, from his written specification

Ours — a guess, clearly labelled

  • Every recipe and every ingredient quantity
  • Every ingredient cost
  • Opening stock levels and par levels
  • Which store each POS category depletes from
Why we guessed loudly

Every sample recipe is flagged in the database and a violet banner sits on the screens saying so. We would far rather he corrects us than assumes we invented his numbers and quietly stops trusting the whole thing.

09

Deliberately not built

This is a demo whose job is to prove the numbers are right, not to be the finished product. What is missing is missing on purpose.

  • Purchasing and receiving. Stock arrives via seeded opening balances instead.
  • Approvals. Drawn on the validation screen, greyed out, clearly inert.
  • Wastage, voids, complimentary and staff meals. The ledger already has movement types for all four; there are no screens.
  • Inter-outlet transfers. Same — modelled, not built.
  • Logging in. One hardcoded user.
  • Anything financial. No payables, receivables, general ledger or statements. Explicitly out of scope.
  • Live POS or Peachtree integration. Files are uploaded by hand.
  • A screen for theoretical consumption. The engine computes it; there is no page yet.
One thing to verify before real use

Two people posting the same import at the same instant is guarded against, but that guard has never actually been exercised — the test database only allows one connection at a time. The design is right; it needs proving against a real PostgreSQL server before go-live. This is written down in the project brief so it cannot be forgotten.

10

Questions only he can answer

These are modelled as settings rather than guessed in code, so his answer is a change of data, not a rebuild. Every assumed row is marked as assumed.

  1. Which sales carry the service charge? It came to 4.9365% of net, so roughly half. Takeaway is the likely reason, but nobody has confirmed it.
  2. Which store does each POS category deplete from? His export carries no outlet dimension at all — nothing on a sales row says whether it came from the bar or the kitchen. Category is the only proxy available. Our least confident guess is CAFE MENU → Bar; traditional coffee may well be its own station.
  3. Are the kitchen and bar really separate stores? His written spec says yes with transfers between them; his workbook merges them. We followed the spec.
  4. How many approval levels, and on which documents?
  5. Do ingredients need yield and trim factors? A kilo of onions is not a kilo of usable onion. Not modelled yet.
  6. Will he split SOFT DRINK and BEER into real products? Until he does, 3.68% of revenue can never be costed.

The one that will bite you

Stop the server before db:reset. The database holds its data directory exclusively. Two processes writing to it at once will corrupt it.

There is no database to install. With no DATABASE_URL set, the app runs on PGlite — real PostgreSQL compiled to run inside the application itself, saving to a .pgdata folder. Point DATABASE_URL at a real server and the identical code runs against it, because the SQL uses no extensions and nothing cloud-specific. That matters because nobody has yet decided whether this lives in the cloud or on a machine at the property.

12

Under the hood, briefly

Next.js and TypeScript on the front, PostgreSQL underneath, Tailwind for styling, SheetJS to read the spreadsheets. Seven database migrations build the schema; nothing needs a database extension.

The two functions everything depends on

costRecipe(recipe, date) walks the tree down to raw ingredients, prices each at the cost version in force on that date, and returns the cost per portion with a full breakdown. If any ingredient has no price on that date it returns nothing at all rather than a smaller number — a partial cost looks like an answer and isn't one.

postSalesImport(import) records the day's revenue and depletes stock for every line that has a recipe, in a single transaction that either completes entirely or does nothing.

A deliberate redundancy

The recipe costing is written twice — once in TypeScript, once in SQL. A test asserts the two agree to the birr on every recipe. Two independent implementations that must match is a cheap and unusually effective check that the arithmetic is right.

What the tests actually prove

  • His real file reconciles to the last decimal, and a one-thousandth of a birr change breaks it
  • Article codes survive as text, leading zeros intact
  • Posting the same file twice writes nothing the second time
  • Changing a cost leaves earlier reports byte-for-byte identical
  • The ledger physically refuses edits and deletions
  • Recipe cycles are caught rather than looped forever
  • Free articles move stock and earn no revenue
  • The code rounds exactly the way PostgreSQL rounds
  • A count adjustment changes quantity without disturbing the average cost

The four moments that matter on Tuesday

If the meeting gets cut short, these are the four things worth showing. Everything else is supporting material.

Moment one — I read your actual file

8,928,373.06 matches his footer exactly. Not a demo file, not a sample. His file, and the system refuses to post anything that doesn't tie.

Moment two — stock moved by itself

348 kilos of beef left the kitchen and nobody typed a number. He sold kitfo; the recipe worked out the beef.

Moment three — history does not move

Butter goes from 900 to 1,150. August says 415.51. July still says 407.18. This is the thing his spreadsheet cannot do, and the reason he is buying software.

Moment four — the honest number

43% coverage. Not 100%, not hidden. And then the curve that turns "write 171 recipes" into "write five and you're at half, twenty-three and you're at eighty percent." A finite job with a visible finish line.

And one thing worth telling him gently

His current spreadsheet under-costs every dish that uses a sub-recipe, because those lines are priced at zero. His Classic Beef Burger is costed at 106.15 and leaves out its own bun. He has been running his margins off numbers that are too low. This system rolls sub-recipes up properly — which means its numbers will be higher than his, and that difference is a feature to explain, not a discrepancy to apologise for.
